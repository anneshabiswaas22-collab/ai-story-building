# Episode 1 teaching notes

Core listening is A1–A2 with slower, clear General American performances. Some jokes intentionally contain harder language; understanding every word is not required to follow the visual story. Level tags are design targets, not an external CEFR certification. B1/B2 learners receive clarification, reasons and conditional advice as optional extensions. This first episode does not claim to teach all four levels comprehensively.

## Meaning behind the jokes

**Filing system:** a way to organize papers so you can find them. Leo teases Dan's mixed notes, not his ability or intelligence. **Corny joke:** a silly or predictable joke; it also sounds related to corn. **Evidence:** information that helps show what happened. **Ingredients:** foods used to make a dish. **Name tag:** a label showing a person's name; Maya imagines soup wearing the wrong label. **Window seat:** a seat beside a window, applied playfully to broccoli. **Change:** money returned after paying too much; also a verb meaning become different or put on different clothes.

## American pronunciation notes

The printed respellings are approximate learner cues. Uppercase indicates stress, not louder shouting. Narration always says the actual word naturally. `tomato` uses the common American tuh-MAY-toh; `potato` puh-TAY-toh; `vegetables` commonly reduces toward VEJ-tuh-buhlz. Keep final consonants audible without adding a vowel after them. `receipt` has a silent p. `mixed` ends with /t/; do not pronounce an extra syllable. `carrots` ends /s/, `tomatoes`, `potatoes` and `onions` end /z/. `wash` and `because` vowels vary among American speakers; do not mark a natural regional vowel wrong merely because it differs from the cue.

## Grammar scope

The carrot lesson introduces a regular plural; it does not mean every English noun takes s. Tomato and potato take -es in the written plural. Broccoli, spinach and corn are normally mass nouns in these shopping sentences. Count units when useful: a head of broccoli, a bag of spinach, two ears of corn. `How many` asks about countable quantities; `How much` asks about price here, and also mass quantities elsewhere. Possessive apostrophe-s marks belonging in Maya's bag, not a plural. `Could you` makes a polite request. `If you are unsure, ask` is a condition followed by advice.

## Speaking and assessment

Each recap has five seconds of real response time, with no narrator competing with the learner. R01–R23 mostly practice imitation and short sentence production. R24 is retrieval: the three names must remain hidden until the answer. Accept understandable speech; model the phrase again rather than labeling accents as bad. A simple after-episode check is to point to five vegetable illustrations, ask for names, then ask the learner to request an item and ask its price. This is optional companion practice outside the 20-minute episode.

## Extension lines for older / stronger learners

- A2: Could I have four carrots, please? How much are they?
- B1: I mixed up the lists because I did not check the headings.
- B1: Could you repeat which vegetables are for the soup?
- B2: If I have misunderstood, could we check the list together?
- B2: I understand why that was confusing. Let's separate the ingredients before we pay.

Use these in a separate practice session or a future episode's dialogue; do not add extra narration to Episode 1 and exceed 20:00.
